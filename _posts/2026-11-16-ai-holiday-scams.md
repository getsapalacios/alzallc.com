# AI holiday scams: two ways scammers can hit your store, and three checks to do this week

*By Alza Services LLC*

A shopper asks an artificial intelligence (AI) chatbot where to buy a product you sell. The chatbot sends her to a site that looks like yours but isn't. She pays, nothing arrives, and she blames you.

This is already happening. A recent report found that popular chatbots sometimes send shoppers to risky sites, including copies of real retailers. It also found that smaller brands were the most likely to get replaced by a scam site in the answer ([Futurism, November 14, 2026](https://futurism.com/artificial-intelligence/ai-chatbots-recommend-scam)).

## What changed this year

AI made this work cheap. Scammers use it to copy small businesses' videos, logos and social posts, then build fake listings and ads that send customers to knockoffs ([MIT AI Incident Database, Incident 706, 2024](https://airisk.mit.edu/ai-incidents/706-scammers-using-ai-to-impersonate-small-businesses)). This month, an identity-verification company warned that AI-made deepfake ads and fake identities are set to target holiday shoppers ([Veriff, via GlobeNewswire, November 12, 2026](https://www.manilatimes.net/2026/11/12/tmt-newswire/globenewswire/ai-driven-scams-are-set-to-target-holiday-shoppers-this-season/2468523)). That company sells identity checks, so read it as a vendor's warning, not a measurement.

The losses are real, though. In 2025, people filed 56,478 complaints with the Federal Bureau of Investigation's (FBI) Internet Crime Complaint Center about non-payment or non-delivery, and reported about $503 million in losses ([FBI IC3 2025 Internet Crime Report, 2025 data](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf)). That category covers all buyers and sellers, not only holiday stores, and the 2025 dollar total was lower than 2024's. It still shows how common "I paid and nothing came" is.

## Two different problems

People say "scam" as if it were one thing. For a store owner it is two.

**A copycat store** is a fake site, ad or chatbot answer that sends your customers somewhere else. Your own accounts are untouched.

**Account takeover** is when a scammer gets into your real store, email or payment account.

The fixes differ. Two-step sign-in (also called multi-factor authentication) stops the second problem and does nothing about the first. So each check below says which problem it answers.

## Check 1: Make your real store easy to verify (copycat store)

Claim your Google Business Profile and any marketplace listings, so the real ones carry your name. Use the same official web address on your website, social profiles, receipts and emails. Say plainly where you sell, with a line like "Our only online store is at this address" on your site and in your order emails.

Your customers should find the same address everywhere they look for you.

## Check 2: Watch for fakes (copycat store)

Search your own business name this week, and ask an AI chatbot where to buy from you. See what your customers see.

The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) lists stolen photos and logos, social media ads and prices that look too good as signs of a fake store ([FTC, November 25, 2024](https://consumer.ftc.gov/node/86798)). Look for those. If you find a copy, take screenshots first.

If a fake version of your store shows up, report it, then tell your customers where the real one is. Report it to the platform where you found it, to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and to the FBI at ic3.gov. Then post your real link on your social pages and in your next email.

## Check 3: Lock your real accounts (account takeover)

Turn on two-step sign-in for your store, your email and your payment account. It takes ten minutes. Add your social media accounts too.

Two-step sign-in asks for a second proof, like a code on your phone, on top of your password. The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) lists requiring it among four basic steps for small businesses ([CISA, April 29, 2024](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/news/during-national-small-business-week-take-steps-secure-your-business)). Start with email. Whoever controls your email can reset the passwords on almost everything else.
