# How property managers can stop chasing tenants for rent, renewals and maintenance updates

If you manage rentals, you spend a large part of every week sending the same messages. Rent is due. Rent is late. Your lease ends in 60 days. Your repair is scheduled for Thursday. None of these messages is hard. Together, they eat your week.

The fix is not working longer hours. It is to have the routine messages go out on time, every time, and hand you only the ones that need a person.

This post is for small property managers, roughly 10 to 150 doors. It shows which tenant messages to automate first, what your options are, and what should always stay with you.

## The real cost of manual tenant follow-up

Here is an illustrative example. A manager with 80 units sends:

- 80 rent reminders a month
- 10 to 15 late-rent notices
- 5 to 7 renewal reminders, each needing 2 or 3 follow-ups
- 20 or more maintenance updates ("we got your request," "the plumber is coming Tuesday," "is it fixed?")

At 2 to 4 minutes per message, plus the time spent finding the right tenant, unit and date, that can add up to 8 to 12 hours a month. *(Illustrative numbers. Count yours for one week to find out.)*

The bigger cost is what slips. A late renewal reminder can mean a vacancy you could have avoided. A repair with no update turns into an angry phone call.

## Which messages to automate first

Start with messages that follow a clear rule: the same trigger, the same timing, the same words every time.

**1. Rent reminders.** A friendly reminder 3 days before rent is due, and again on the due date, with the tenant's name, unit and amount filled in.

**2. Late-rent notices.** If payment has not posted by the grace date, send a polite notice. If it still has not posted 3 days later, flag it for you. A person should handle anything past that point, because the next step may have legal rules in your state.

**3. Lease renewal reminders.** A reminder at 90, 60 and 30 days before the lease ends, asking one clear question: "Do you plan to renew?" Replies are sorted into "yes," "no" and "no answer," and you see the "no answer" list each week.

**4. Maintenance updates.** An instant confirmation when a request comes in, the date when a vendor is scheduled, and a short "Is everything working?" when the job is marked done. If the tenant says no, it comes straight to you.

**5. Move-in and move-out checklists.** The same checklist at the same point every time, with reminders until it is done.

## You already have property management software. Why is this still on you?

Most managers already pay for property management software. So why are you still sending these messages by hand?

Because the software sends what it is told. It is a system of record: it holds your leases, payments and work orders. It does not decide when to remind a tenant, write the message in your voice, chase the tenant who never replied, or connect to your email, texts and calendar. Someone has to set up those rules and keep them working. In most small offices, that someone is you.

## Your options

Here are the four common ways to get tenant follow-up off your plate, with the honest trade-offs.

**1. Buy more or different software.** Switching systems or adding a new tool can help. But new software brings setup work, data moves and surprise costs. In one survey of buyers who regretted a software purchase, 33% said it cost more than expected and 32% blamed slow or hard implementation. Smaller firms reported more regret than larger ones (Gartner Digital Markets, 2023). Some tools are also built for larger portfolios, with minimum sizes or long contracts.

*Software is the right choice when* you have no system of record yet. Get one first.

**2. Hire a virtual assistant or office admin.** A person can use judgment and handle phone calls. But they work only the hours you pay for, and training, checking their work and turnover stay with you.

*A person is the right choice when* most of the work needs judgment on every message.

**3. Pay an agency or freelancer to set it up.** You get the rules built without learning the tool. When something changes and it breaks, no one owns the fix.

*An agency is the right choice when* you have staff who can maintain it after the handoff.

**4. Keep doing it yourself.** The checklist below makes it faster. It still costs your week.

*Doing it yourself is the right choice when* you manage only a few units.

## What should always stay with a person

Automation should handle the routine. It should not make judgment calls. Keep these with you or your team:

- Any decision about late fees, payment plans or evictions
- Replies from an upset tenant
- Anything involving safety, such as gas, water leaks, no heat or security issues
- Fair housing questions, or anything where wording carries legal weight
- The first message to a new owner or a tenant with a complicated situation

A good setup makes these easier to spot, because they are no longer buried under 100 routine messages.

## A simple checklist to start this week

You can do this even before you automate anything:

1. **Count your messages.** For one week, tally every tenant message you send by type: rent, late, renewal, maintenance, other.
2. **Pick the biggest pile.** That is your first workflow to automate.
3. **Write the message once.** Create one template you are happy with.
4. **Set the timing.** Write down exactly when it should go out, such as "3 days before the due date."
5. **Decide the hand-off rule.** Write down which replies need a person.

With those five things written down, you have the plan for your first automated workflow.
